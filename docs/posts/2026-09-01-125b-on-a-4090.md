## Inference Demo

## 1. The model doesn't fit

I have a 24 GB RTX 4090, a Ryzen 7800X3D, 64 GB of DDR5 and a 1.9 TB NVMe drive. I wanted to serve Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, a 125B mixture-of-experts model, on that machine. Its weights exceed VRAM and RAM combined.

[oom-inference](https://github.com/jomcgi-org/homelab/tree/main/projects/oom-inference) is the Rust/CUDA engine that came out of that constraint. This post follows the choices that made it usable for long documents and code edits. Updated October 6, 2026.

| Recorded request | Input tokens | First token | Decode |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Read an incident report | 21,877 | 6.98 s | 46.0 tok/s |
| Rewrite a source file | 23,725 | 7.24 s | 43.9 tok/s |

Both recordings come from five measured runs after two warmups, with warm expert tiers and fresh prefixes: zero cached prompt tokens. The replay's prefill rate is input tokens divided by client time to first token, including transport and request overhead: 3,134 and 3,276 tok/s respectively. Decode excludes that wait.

> **What these numbers don't show.** They are latency and example output from one machine. The research graph has not been checked against its source report, and the coding recording contains no compile or test result. Neither recording has a speculation-off control, so no speedup can be credited to a draft source, and output quality has not been benchmarked yet. The research request was also chosen partly by its output, as described below. Later sections point back here instead of repeating this.

## 2. Where it started

In August and September I experimented with [FreeToken](https://github.com/FlashML-org/FreeToken), FlashML's engine for running MoE models beyond VRAM. Its pinned-memory expert tier and GPU cache were the starting point for my [disk-tier fork](https://github.com/jomcgi-org/freetoken-fork). The version I initially tested would not start this model on my 64 GB host; that is a historical observation, and upstream has continued changing.

The fork taught me how easily pinning too much RAM could starve the page cache, how expensive whole-layer transfers were for small requests, and how quickly a fixed hot-expert profile went stale. Its [experiment log][historical-results] records those configurations and the failures. They belong to that implementation and hardware setup: most of the fork's benchmarks ran on an L4 with a 64 GiB host-memory cap.

oom-inference became a separate engine with an explicit storage path, reference checks and an interactive server. The rest of this post describes that engine as of October 6.

For a before-and-after on the demo request, the research recording embeds a fork run of the same request from earlier that day (build `6a92a8f`): 43.3 tok/s decode and 6.93 s to first token. The engine's recording is 46.0 tok/s and 6.98 s, so decode is about 6% faster and first-token time is level. The recording does not capture the fork's runtime settings, so this is a same-request reference, not a controlled comparison.

## 3. Decide where every expert lives

A token passes through every decoder layer, but each MoE router selects only 10 of its 512 experts. Most expert weights sit idle for that token. Keeping likely experts close to the GPU matters more than loading the whole model at once.

![One layer: 10 of 512 routed experts run for a token](figures/oom-expert-routing.svg)

| Key | Part |
|---|---|
| 1 | The router scores the token and picks 10 of the layer's 512 routed experts. |
| 2 | The expert pool. Heavy squares are the experts selected for this token; faint ones sit idle. Their positions are illustrative. |
| 3 | Dense and shared-expert work runs for every token whatever the route. |

Each token uses a small subset of each layer's expert weights, so frequently used experts can stay close to the GPU. The 10-of-512 ratio applies to routed experts in one layer; it does not mean only 2% of the whole model runs. The model's per-layer embedding (PLE) is separate: it gathers selected n-gram table rows rather than loading the whole table, as described below.

The [converted format][weight-format] stores all of one expert's weights and scales in one aligned record. A miss needs one read and one host-to-device copy. Conversion rearranges the released bytes without requantizing them.

The [documented source checkpoint][release-inventory] contains about 134.26 GB (125.04 GiB) of text-model tensors after excluding vision weights: 67.95 GB of decoder experts, 5.03 GB of MTP experts, 10.08 GB of dense weights and 51.20 GB of FP8 per-layer embedding tables. Here GB means decimal gigabytes. The converter's alignment adds about 0.10 GB. [`release_inventory.py`][inventory-script] reproduces the inventory by reading the pinned release's tensor headers with the converter's classification rules. The recordings pin engine builds but do not record a checkpoint hash.

Where those bytes live decides the speed. A GPU-resident configuration keeps every expert in VRAM; this engine keeps a hot subset there and moves or computes only what a token needs.

![A GPU-resident baseline against the three expert tiers](figures/oom-residency.svg)

| Key | Part |
|---|---|
| 1 | A GPU-resident configuration: dense weights, sequence state and every expert sit in VRAM. Other engines also support offload; this is the baseline for what a larger card avoids. |
| 2 | Here VRAM holds dense weights and sequence state, and the hot expert cache gets what remains. |
| 3 | Pinned host RAM caches expert records within a fixed budget. A host hit is copied to VRAM over PCIe. |
| 4 | NVMe holds every expert. A miss is a direct read into the pinned arena. |
| 5 | During decode, the CPU can compute a host-resident expert and return a small output row instead of copying its weights. |

A smaller GPU can serve the model by moving or computing only the needed experts, paying transfer and cache-miss costs. The figure below shows the same paths in implementation detail.

![Expert records and PLE rows take different paths](figures/oom-memory-paths.svg)

| Key | Part |
|---|---|
| 1 | VRAM holds dense weights, sequence state and a cache of expert records. Expert-cache chunks can be returned when growing sequence state needs the space. |
| 2 | A bounded pinned host arena caches expert records. Host hits can be copied to VRAM over PCIe without another disk read. |
| 3 | Expert misses use `O_DIRECT` reads through `io_uring`, landing directly in the pinned arena. This path bypasses the page cache. |
| 4 | During decode, suitable host-resident experts can run on the CPU alongside the GPU's resident experts. Only their small output rows need to move. |
| 5 | PLE is separate: buffered `io_uring` reads gather the required n-gram table rows into host buffers, then upload them to the GPU. This path can use the page cache. |

### Context spends the same VRAM

The context cap can be set to 262,144 tokens, including the prompt and generated output; the server defaults to 32,768. A [recorded 255k-token prompt][context-result] completed on the 4090 at 1,171 prefill tok/s and 22.8 decode tok/s with warm tiers and compressed KV. It passed the needle-retrieval checks at 10%, 50% and 90% depth. That is evidence the window works, with a limited retrieval check.

![Context state grows with attention layers and shrinks the expert cache](figures/oom-context-state.svg)

| Key | Part |
|---|---|
| 1 | Gated DeltaNet (GDN) layers carry a fixed-size recurrent state however long the context grows. |
| 2 | Qwen Sparse Attention (QSA) layers choose which history rows a query reads, but they still store the whole KV history, so that state grows with context. |
| 3 | Shorter context, schematically: dense weights and a small sequence state leave most of VRAM to expert slots. |
| 4 | Longer context: the same dense weights, more sequence state, fewer expert slots. The bars are illustrative, not to scale. |

The hybrid architecture reduces context-state growth, but longer contexts still leave less room for hot experts. In this model three GDN layers are followed by one QSA layer, repeated through the 48 decoder layers.

The engine does not reserve the entire cap at startup. [Attention caches start at 2,048 tokens][context-allocation] and grow as needed, returning expert-cache chunks before they expand. A shorter context can leave more experts in VRAM; a longer one can require more host copies and disk reads. The capacity and latency trade happens within the same process.

Freed memory is reclaimed for expert slots after prefill cleanup and when sessions are replaced. Rewinding a sequence alone keeps its allocated buffers, and newly reclaimed slots still need warming. A 256k cap does not mean every request pays for 256k, or that shrinking a prompt instantly restores the earlier decode rate.

### Keep transfers worth their cost

The [host tier][host-tier] gives expert caching a memory budget I can account for. It also gives me responsibility for eviction, in-flight reads and safe slot reuse. The page cache no longer decides which expert records stay resident.

Copying a decoder expert costs about 2.7 MB over PCIe. For a one-token step, the CPU can sometimes compute a host-resident expert while the GPU is busy and return roughly 10 KB of output. The engine caps that work per layer. A repeated expert is promoted to VRAM; a one-off route can stay on the host. CPU execution earns its place only when it saves more transfer time than it adds in compute.

| One 2.7 MB expert record | Cost |
|---|---|
| Already in VRAM | No transfer |
| Host-tier hit, copied over PCIe | About 110 µs, with the GPU waiting |
| Host-tier hit, computed on 8 CPU cores (one-token decode) | About 70 µs, and only a 10 KB row moves |
| NVMe miss | A direct read into the pinned arena, then the copy above. I have not measured this latency inside the engine; the fork's earlier figure belonged to that implementation |

The design in this section follows that ladder: keep what is hot in VRAM, keep a bounded warm tier in RAM, and compute on the host when copying would cost more.

The model's [per-layer embedding lookup][ple-source] needs much smaller, scattered rows. Their addresses follow from the input tokens, so they can be gathered before the layer consumes them. Sharing the expert-record cache would give these two access patterns the same eviction and I/O granularity. I kept them separate.

## 4. Read long prompts layer by layer

**What it bought:** warm 32k prefill fell from 10.30 s to 9.40 s once stage-ahead copies were submitted a few at a time.

Prefill processes the prompt before producing the first token. A long prompt routes to most of a layer's experts. If I run one small chunk through the whole model, then repeat for the next chunk, those chunks keep loading the same layers.

oom-inference uses layer-major prefill. It holds the prompt's intermediate states, runs a layer across chunks, fetches the union of their routed experts, then advances to the next layer. A separate one-layer staging area limits how much the sweep displaces experts used by decode; staging ahead can still borrow the main cache's coldest slots. The next layer's predicted experts load while the current layer computes; actual routing still decides what runs.

Keeping those intermediate states takes memory. Very long prompts are split into the fewest windows that fit, and each additional window makes another sweep through the experts. Warm tiers help, but a warm prefill can still read gigabytes from NVMe.

One improvement came from submitting *less* work ahead. The GPU has one host-to-device copy engine. Queuing roughly 1.2 GB for the next layer made copies needed by the current layer wait behind it, even on separate CUDA streams. I changed staging to submit eight records at a time as earlier copies completed. The [recorded experiment][architecture] reduced warm 32k prefill from 10.30 to 9.40 seconds; 95k changed from 28.1 to 27.4 seconds, with decode unchanged.

That measurement is separate from the demos. A prefix-cache hit would skip much of this work, so prefill experiments need fresh prefixes as well as a stated expert-cache condition.

## 5. Get more tokens from each pass

**What it bought:** prompt lookup raised file-edit output rates by 22–40%, and keeping MTP caught up across lookup steps recovered acceptance from 76% to 83% and decode from 43.4 to 45.6 tok/s on a warm report workload.

Decode repeatedly reads the same dense weights and fetches a few experts. Verifying several proposed tokens in one pass can amortize that work.

The checkpoint includes a multi-token-prediction head, or MTP. It proposes a next token using the preceding model state. The main model verifies the proposal and keeps the accepted prefix plus its own next token. Rejected positions must be removed from the attention cache, and the recurrent layers must restore and replay their state for the tokens kept.

Prompt lookup offers another source of proposals. When the latest tokens match an earlier span of the prompt or output, the engine proposes the following text from that span. File rewrites contain lots of unchanged code, so a seven-token lookup draft can be more useful there than on new prose. If lookup finds no match, MTP supplies the draft.

Cheap proposals still need affordable verification. An early eight-token path fell onto prefill kernels and repeatedly dequantized dense weights for matrix multiplication. The current short verification paths use decode kernels. In the [lookup experiments][architecture], this configuration improved file-edit output rates by 22–40%, was level on tests, and lost 2–9% on prose, quotations and a short diff. Acceptance and verification cost both matter.

Combining the two draft sources created another bug: lookup could keep producing tokens without calling MTP, leaving its attention cache behind. The engine now retains the last 256 positions' final residuals in a roughly 10 MB ring. Before its next proposal, MTP catches up on the intervening positions. In a separate warm report experiment that recovered MTP acceptance from 76% to 83% and decode from 43.4 to 45.6 tok/s. Those development measurements are separate from the recorded demo above.

The replay's draft colours are approximate. The server offers one MTP draft per step, so a two-token arrival group almost always means that draft was kept. Lookup steps of three or more tokens can be partial accepts, and grouping by arrival gap can occasionally merge steps. Exact acceptance needs proposal/acceptance counters, and a speedup attributable to lookup needs matched runs with each draft source enabled and disabled.

## 6. Check the model before trading precision

An incorrect kernel can still write plausible text. I started with [reference fixtures][testing] from the model's Hugging Face implementation: individual layer stages, then the whole chain through the final logits. The reference's own BF16-versus-FP32 differences set numerical error budgets. Separate tests cover speculative rewinds, saved sequences and prefix checkpoints. TLA+ models check that asynchronous tiering cannot expose a partially installed or reused expert slot.

Preserving checkpoint bytes during conversion is only one part of that. Runtime arithmetic and the KV cache make further choices:

| Setting | Choice |
|---|---|
| Default dense weights | BF16, as released |
| Default expert prefill | Exact activation splitting into BF16 terms, FP32 accumulation |
| Default attention prefill | FP32 |
| Default KV cache | `k8v6`: 8-bit keys and 6-bit values; about 3x less KV memory, with output shift within fp32's own rounding noise at 32k to 95k tokens ([#6830](https://github.com/jomcgi-org/homelab/issues/6830)) |
| Recorded demos | FP8 dense weights and BF16-rounded expert/attention prefill, plus `k8v6` |

The compressed cache leaves more VRAM for experts as context grows. FP8 dense weights save more space and memory traffic, but their next-token distributions shift measurably against the higher-precision path. CPU/GPU rounding, routing and compressed caches can also make greedy output vary between runs. The [precision notes][architecture] describe those limits.

A deeper benchmark should compare the original base model, a reference execution of the same quantized checkpoint, the normal runtime settings and the faster settings used here. Matching prompts and decoding conditions would separate checkpoint quantization from runtime changes. Existing numerical checks and small task sets are useful development gates, but they are not that comparison.

## 7. Make it useful between requests

I wanted ordinary clients to use it, so the server exposes OpenAI- and Anthropic-compatible APIs with streaming, chat templates and tool-call parsing.

**What it bought:** a follow-up question about a 32k-token document gets its first token in 0.64 seconds from the live checkpoint, against 13.1 seconds of prefill.

The [prefix experiments][architecture] measured that on a new question over the same 32k-token documents, using the faster runtime configuration: 0.64 seconds from the live checkpoint and 0.95 seconds from a stored entry. The 13.1 seconds is the cold first question. The stored snapshot was 1.1 GB ([measured in #6859][prefix-snapshot]); its restore time can benefit from the page cache and warm experts.

Repeated context is worth keeping, so the engine has its own persistent prefix-state store. It can resume the latest live GPU sequence or, with `--prefix-store-dir`, restore a local snapshot; file reads can hit the OS page cache. Snapshotting transfers state to host memory before a background writer saves it. Restoring moves state back to the device, so reuse has a transfer cost as well as a storage budget. A new question often changes only the end of a long document prompt. Because recurrent layers cannot simply truncate their state, prefill records checkpoints containing that state as well as the attention position. A matching request restores the longest usable checkpoint and reads only the remaining tokens.

With [continuous batching](https://github.com/jomcgi-org/homelab/pull/6889), several requests can share a step's dense-weight reads and the union of its experts, while keeping their sequence state separate. The current server defaults to two streams. Admission accounts for each active sequence's prompt and output budget; requests that do not fit queue. Higher-concurrency experiments improved aggregate output and queueing, while slowing each stream; a new prefill can still pause existing decodes. Slicing prefill reduced those pauses but repeated expert sweeps and delayed the new request. That remains a tradeoff exposed to the caller.

The current implementation is text-only Qwen `qwen4_exp` on CUDA. Model and backend interfaces leave room for other families and hardware, but those ports are work still to do.

## 8. Ideas that did not pan out

Measured and not kept, each from the [architecture notes][architecture]:

| Idea | Result |
|---|---|
| Draft width growing from one to three tokens while recent drafts were kept | Tied the demo (46.4 against 46.3 tok/s) and lost 3 to 7% on file edits. Fixed two- and three-token drafts ran the demo at 45.6 and 42.8 tok/s |
| `--kv-placement host` | Identical output and 9% faster decode at 95k, but 4% slower at 32k and slower at every length once `--dense fp8` frees the VRAM anyway |
| A larger host tier | Cut disk reads 19% and did not move the prefill stall; the copy-engine queue was the cause |
| Grouping adjacent prefill tokens to share key tiles | A key in a 4-token group's union is seen by about 1.2 of them, so shared tiles mostly compute masked-out scores |
| Residual rings of 32, 64 and 256 rows | Measured the same on the edit workloads |

## 9. How close does the smaller machine get?

The 4090 recordings sustain 44–46 tok/s after reading about 22–24k tokens. That is useful interactive output from hardware that cannot hold the model. Published results on a 96 GB RTX PRO 6000 show the headroom available when the expert weights can stay in VRAM.

These are author-reported scale references from the same model family, with different checkpoints, precisions and workloads. They are not a controlled ranking.

| Published configuration | Speculation | Decode |
|---|---|---:|
| [vLLM, primitive-ai NVFP4][vllm-result], 8k input / 512 output, cache-free | Off | 74.4 tok/s |
| [SGLang, NVFP4 + W4 PLE][sglang-paired], paired report, 512 output | Off | 97.9 tok/s |
| Same SGLang report, tuned candidate | MTP + other tuning | 144.6 tok/s |
| [SGLang, later tuned stack][sglang-later], four workloads, 8,192 output | MTP | 179.4–192.7 tok/s |
| [llama.cpp, UD-Q4_K_XL][llama-result], 22.7k prompt | Off | 59.7 tok/s |
| oom-inference research replay, 4090 | MTP + lookup | 46.0 tok/s |

The 97.9/144.6 pair comes from one warm, concurrency-one campaign. Its author notes that KV capacity, memory settings and idle conditions also changed, so the difference cannot be assigned entirely to MTP. The later 179–193 results use long reasoning outputs and different sampling. The vLLM run keeps a roughly 95 GB BF16 PLE table in host RAM; the SGLang and llama.cpp rows make other PLE, weight and KV choices. Calling all of them “NVFP4” would hide material differences.

The question for me is how much of that experience I can retain with a quarter of the VRAM and a bounded host-memory budget. A matched comparison still needs the same inputs, output lengths, sampling, cache conditions and quality checks. Without a speculation-off control, as noted at the top, there is no measured multiplier to remove from its rate.

To try it, the [quickstart](https://github.com/jomcgi-org/homelab/tree/main/projects/oom-inference#use) is `oominf convert` once, then `oominf serve --model model.oom` for the OpenAI and Anthropic APIs; `oominf bench` reports decode speed and tier statistics. The recordings above used a 4090, 64 GB of RAM and a 1.9 TB NVMe drive, with warm expert tiers, so expect the first requests after a cold start to be slower than the demos. `--dense fp8` and the two bf16 precision flags are opt-in; read section 6 before turning them on. The [architecture][architecture] and [testing guide][testing] explain the rest.

For me, running a model this size at home means the storage path is now part of the model's design, not an afterthought. I want to see how the faster settings hold up under a deeper quality benchmark before I trust them for more than demos.
