## 1. FreeToken

[FreeToken](https://github.com/FlashML-org/FreeToken) is FlashML's engine for running mixture-of-experts models. It serves models larger than VRAM by keeping the experts in pinned RAM and caching the hot ones in VRAM.
This does not work for my node with 24GB VRAM + 64GB RAM, this post (& [fork](https://github.com/jomcgi-org/freetoken-fork)) is an expirement where I spill experts to disk to accomodate a larger than memory model.

The weights don't fit in VRAM or RAM and I can't convince myself to upgrade in this RAM economy so here we are spilling to disk.

The peaks are on a workload that matches the routing profile. The sustained figures are on mixed traffic, and those are the ones to quote.

The GPU never gets warm. 138 W is under a third of what the card can draw. The step is bound by the host: RAM bandwidth, page faults, and the CPU doing the decode for whichever experts are not on the GPU.

## 4. What each tier costs

Five findings, in the order they were measured. Each one moved a number in the table above.

### 4.1 The page cache is a tier

FreeToken pins expert banks into host memory with a byte budget. On day one the budget was 52 GB of the 64. That left nothing for the kernel to cache the 57 GB of disk-resident data in, so every expert read from NVMe was a fresh fault: 96,000 major faults per decode step, 3.8 tokens a second.

Dropping the budget to 40 GB pinned less and ran three times faster. The pin budget competes with the page cache for the same RAM, and the page cache is what makes the disk tier work. Size the budget to leave the spill working set cached.

### 4.2 Disk is free for expert banks and fatal for lookup tables

An expert bank on disk is a read-only mmap of the weight file. Routing happens before the expert compute, so once the router has picked its experts for a step, the pages they need are known. Dedupe those pages and issue one `MADV_WILLNEED` for the batch. At steady state that runs at under ten major faults per step. Disk-resident experts cost nothing you can measure.

The per-token lookup table is different. It is touched on every token, at random rows, from inside a CUDA graph. On a cloud vGPU any file-backed mapping of it cost a flat 105 ms per step, and no amount of pre-touching helped: the GPU re-faulted the mapping on every graph replay.

That turned out to be a vGPU artifact. On bare metal with the open NVIDIA driver, heterogeneous memory management (the GPU reading host file mappings directly) is the fastest backend for the table. Switching to it nearly doubled four-stream throughput, 14 to 26.5, and freed the pinned cache the table had been using, so more expert banks fit under the same budget.

### 4.3 Prefill must never copy a whole layer

The first prefill of a six-token prompt took 17 minutes. That was on a rented L4 with a 64 GB memory cap standing in for this box, before the card was free to test on.

The upstream prefill path streams whole expert layers to the GPU. For a disk-resident layer that meant paging in the entire bank, 59.5 GB across all spilled layers, per prefill batch, regardless of prompt length. The fix routes prefill for disk layers through the CPU executor, which only touches the experts the router selected. 441 tokens went from 17 minutes to 29 seconds, then to 5.5 seconds once the lookup table stopped competing for RAM.

The second half was overlap. With some layers pinned and some on disk, the pinned layers can overlap their PCIe transfer with compute while the disk layers prefill on the CPU and chain the next pinned layer's prefetch. Warm prefill went from 54 to 116 tokens a second, and the config that was best for single-stream stopped being worst for concurrency.

![A prefill chunk: what is known before the forward and what is read once](figures/prefill-chunk.svg)

| Key | Part |
|---|---|
| 1 | Before the forward: the chunk's n-gram ids are computed from its input tokens and deduped. Nothing about them depends on the forward |
| 2 | One read each: the deduped table rows arrive in one coalesced read into a pinned GPU bank; after each layer's routing, its expert rows are populated into the page cache with real reads instead of advisory hints |
| 3 | Forward: the lookup resolves against the pinned bank by compact local id while the experts compute. Major faults per chunk fell from about 3.6 million to a few thousand |

### 4.4 Routing is Zipf, so tier per expert

Up to this point residency was decided per layer: a layer is pinned or it is on disk. But the router does not spread load evenly across experts. A small head of experts takes most of the routes and a long tail takes the rest.

Pinning the hot rows (about 6 GB, covering 72% of routes) and sending them through the GPU's expert slot cache, while the cold tail stays on CPU decode, took single-stream from 19.5 to 23.1 and eight-stream from 34 to 42.2. Distinct experts hitting the CPU per step fell from 58 to 18. That broke a ceiling I had calculated as a DDR bandwidth wall. The wall was real. Moving the head of the distribution off the CPU tier moved it.

The optimum was found by a two-dimensional sweep: 40 GB pinned plus a 6 GB hot set. Both neighbours (44/4 and 36/8) lose. The same three-way balance as 4.1, one more tier in.

![One decode step through one expert layer at the final config](figures/decode-step.svg)

| Key | Part |
|---|---|
| 1 | Route: the router picks this token's experts from 512 in the layer. The n-gram ids are already known from the last few tokens |
| 2 | Sort by residency: each chosen expert is hot (resident on the GPU), pinned (in host memory), or cold (on the NVMe, possibly in the page cache). About 58 distinct experts a step before the hot set, 18 on the CPU after |
| 3 | Move: pinned experts stream over PCIe into a GPU slot; cold experts get one deduped willneed and are computed by the CPU out of the page cache; the GPU reads the n-gram rows straight through the file mapping |
| 4 | Compute and tally: the GPU and CPU partial outputs sum, and every routed expert bumps a decayed counter |
| A | RTX 4090: expert slot cache with the hot set resident, KV cache |
| B | Pinned host memory, 40 GB of expert banks |
| C | Page cache |
| D | NVMe: expert banks 63.5 GB, lookup table 27 GB |
| E | CPU MoE executor |

### 4.5 The hierarchy can tune itself

The hot set in 4.4 came from a captured routing profile. When live traffic drifted from the capture, the hot-hit rate fell from 72% to 62%, and the gain with it.

Replacing the static profile with decayed per-expert counters and bounded background swaps (retire, copy, flip, so no mapping is ever torn) recovered the hit rate from 62.6% to 73.3% over four adaptation windows on the drifted workload, while the static profile stayed at 62%. It also means a cold start works: every expert begins on the CPU tier and the hot set assembles itself, so deployment needs no capture step.

![How one hot-set slot changes hands without a stall](figures/hot-set-swap.svg)

| Key | Part |
|---|---|
| 1 | Tick: every 1,000 steps the decayed counters are re-ranked under the same 6 GB budget. One expert crosses into the hot set, one drops out |
| 2 | Stage: a background thread copies the newly hot rows from the NVMe into staging, at most 0.5 GB a tick. The retiring expert keeps serving from where it was |
| 3 | Flip: at a step boundary the mapping points at the staged row and the retired slot is free. No route ever sees a half-installed row |
| A | GPU slot cache, one hot slot of the 6 GB |
| D | NVMe expert banks |

## 5. Fault diagnosis

Five things that should have worked and measured as losses. The mechanism is still in the fork, off by default, with the reason next to the flag.

| Symptom | Cause | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| GPU-fetch decode (pull routed experts over PCIe instead of CPU decode) drops single-stream to 10 | About 990 expert fills a step at 2.6 MB each thrash the slot cache across 43 layers | Keep it off below 48 GB VRAM. It is the cold-tail path for a 96 GB card |
| Hybrid backend (split between PCIe fetch and CPU) loses to plain offload, 13.8 vs 19.5 | On this host, CPU compute of an expert beats fetching it | Same as above |
| `userfaultfd` pager gives 13 faults a step and is still slower than `madvise` | Both are page-granular; the pager's wins are all capacity, none throughput | Use it when the banks are bigger than RAM, and give it a budget that covers the working set. Under-budgeted it evicts on every install (1.6 million evictions, single-stream 10) |
| Lookahead prefetch of next-step experts makes things worse | Next-step routing is 48% predictable, so the prefetch nearly doubles advised pages for half a hit | Off |
| Multi-token prediction (speculative decode) is net negative even at 72% acceptance, 12.6 vs 18.0 | Speculation pays when the marginal token is cheap. With any share of the decode on the CPU tier, each drafted token costs full price | Closed on this box. Untested on all-pinned or big-VRAM configs |
| Sixteen concurrent streams do not fit | The linear-attention layers carry per-slot state that scales with concurrency | Eight is the 24 GB sweet spot. Twelve peaks below eight |

## 6. What is left

Step timing at the final config, eight streams: the CPU phases take 57 to 100 ms per step, the GPU phase 19 to 40 ms, and 20 to 45 ms of that is already overlapped. The engines were never idle by accident. The remaining overlap needs the scheduler to allow more than one forward in flight at a time, which is a contract change I have documented and not made.

What the software program did to the hardware shopping list:

- **Another 64 GB of RAM** went from obvious to nice. The old case was 9 to 36 single-stream. Now it is 23 to about 36.
- **An 8-channel EPYC board** lost its argument for this model class. The case was that aggregate throughput is DDR-bound, so buy channels. Section 4.4 moved the hot experts to the GPU instead.
- **A 96 GB RTX PRO 6000** gained one. The hot working set fits in VRAM entirely, GPU-fetch becomes the cold-tail path, and the concurrency cap lifts with it.

## 7. Why a 4090

At every shape I measured, renting a cloud GPU to serve this model costs more per token than the hosted API for a comparable model. The fork earns its keep on a card you already own, serving traffic you would rather not send anywhere, at the power draw of a bright bulb.

The tier has been verified on one model family. The quality gate is smoke-level. The peaks are profile-matched. Everything else in this post is measured on the box under the desk.

Every mechanism in this post is built on upstream FreeToken's per-layer residency seams, its CPU MoE executor, and its Flash-Next support. The fork is on GitHub as a branch of it.
